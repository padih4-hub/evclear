The growth has been anything but smooth — it comes in jumps. The Tesla Model 3 launch in 2019 caused the first visible spike. The Model Y launch in mid-2022 caused another. The arrival of affordable Chinese EVs from BYD, MG, and others caused the most recent surge.

April 2026 hit a record 16.4% EV market share — nearly one in six new cars sold. That's up from essentially zero a decade ago.

The pattern follows a classic S-curve adoption model. We're currently in the steep part of that curve — the phase where growth accelerates fastest and mainstream buyers start switching in large numbers. The fuel price crisis in 2026, combined with prices dropping to under $25,000 for some models, has pulled even more buyers in earlier than expected.

### What Happens to the EV Market at 80% Penetration

When a new technology reaches this kind of penetration, a few things happen simultaneously that matter for buyers today:

**Resale values stabilise** — right now used EV prices are uncertain because the market is new. At 80% adoption, used EVs are as mainstream as used petrol cars today. Resale values become predictable and generally strong for quality models.

**Charging infrastructure catches up** — at current adoption rates, charging networks are being built ahead of demand. By the time we approach 80%, public charging will be as ubiquitous as petrol stations. The infrastructure concern disappears.

**Petrol car resale crashes** — this is the flip side. As EVs become the norm, petrol cars become increasingly undesirable on the used market. The people waiting to buy a petrol car "one last time" will face a resale problem at the end of their ownership.

**Service network shifts** — traditional mechanics will increasingly pivot to EV servicing or lose relevance. EV-specific service networks will be mature and well established.

The brands making the biggest contribution to Australia's EV growth trajectory right now:

### What Could Slow the Trajectory

The 80% by 2030 scenario assumes the current growth rate continues. A few things could slow it:

**Ute and 4WD holdouts** — the Toyota HiLux and Ford Ranger are Australia's best selling vehicles and there are no compelling electric alternatives yet. The Toyota HiLux BEV exists but has limited range and a significant price premium. Until electric utes become genuinely competitive, a segment of buyers will stick with petrol.

**Apartment charging** — the biggest practical barrier to EV adoption for urban Australians without a garage. Until body corporates and local governments make kerbside and apartment charging routine, a meaningful segment of buyers will hesitate.

**Economic conditions** — if household budgets tighten significantly, discretionary spending on new cars drops. This affects EVs and petrol cars equally but EVs have higher upfront costs which can make them more sensitive to economic downturns.

None of these are reasons to expect the trajectory to reverse — but they could moderate the pace.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Will Australia really reach 80% EV sales by 2030?**

Based on current growth trends the trajectory points to 75-80% by 2029-2030. This assumes continued exponential growth similar to the past 15 years. Real world obstacles like ute adoption and apartment charging could moderate the pace.

**Does high EV adoption affect resale values?**

Yes — positively for EVs and negatively for petrol cars. As EVs become mainstream, used EV values stabilise while petrol car resale values are likely to decline as demand shifts.

**Should I buy an EV now or wait for better models?**

For most buyers, now makes more financial sense than waiting. The FBT exemption is being wound back, petrol prices are rising, and current models are genuinely excellent. Future models will be better but they'll also come with less financial support.

**Which EVs are driving Australia's growth?**

BYD, Tesla, Kia, Hyundai, and MG are the dominant brands. The BYD Sealion 7 is currently the best selling EV in Australia.
