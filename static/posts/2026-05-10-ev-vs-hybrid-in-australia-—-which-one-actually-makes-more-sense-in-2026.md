If you're trying to decide between an EV and a hybrid in Australia right now, you're not alone. It's one of the most common questions people ask before making the switch — and the answer isn't as simple as "EVs are better."
It depends on how you drive, where you live, and what you're trying to get out of the car. Here's the honest breakdown.

What's the Actual Difference?
Before the comparison, let's be clear on what we're comparing.
A hybrid (HEV) uses a petrol engine combined with a small electric motor and battery. The battery charges itself through regenerative braking and the engine — you never plug it in. The Toyota Camry Hybrid and Toyota RAV4 Hybrid are the most common examples in Australia.
A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) has a larger battery you can charge from a power point, giving you a short electric-only range (typically 40-80km) before the petrol engine takes over. Examples include the Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV and BYD Seal U DM-i.
A full Battery Electric Vehicle (BEV) runs entirely on electricity. No petrol engine, no fuel costs, no exhaust. Examples include the Tesla Model 3, BYD Seal, and Hyundai Ioniq 6.
For this comparison we're focusing mainly on BEV vs hybrid — since that's the real decision most Australians face.

The Numbers — Running Costs Over 3 Years
Let's use a real comparison. You're choosing between a Toyota RAV4 Hybrid ($43,990) and a BYD Sealion 7 ($54,900) — both popular family SUVs.
Assume 15,000km per year, petrol at $2.00/litre, electricity at 30c/kWh.
RAV4 Hybrid running costs (3 years)

Fuel: ~6L/100km × 45,000km × $2.00 = $5,400
Servicing: approximately $3,000 over 3 years
Total running cost: ~$8,400

BYD Sealion 7 running costs (3 years)

Charging: ~18kWh/100km × 45,000km × $0.30 = $2,430
Servicing: approximately $1,500 over 3 years
Total running cost: ~$3,930

Saving on running costs: approximately $4,470 over 3 years
But the Sealion 7 costs $10,910 more upfront. So on running costs alone, the EV doesn't fully close the gap in 3 years.
Here's where it changes: the novated lease. If you're an employee with access to salary packaging, the FBT exemption applies to the BYD but not the hybrid. That $13,000-$18,000 tax saving completely flips the equation — the EV becomes significantly cheaper overall.

What About Charging?
This is the most common reason people hesitate on EVs — and it's worth being honest about.
If you live in a house with a garage or driveway, home charging is easy and cheap. You plug in overnight and wake up to a full car every morning. The "range anxiety" most people worry about rarely materialises in daily life.
If you live in an apartment without dedicated parking, charging is genuinely more complicated. Public charging networks are improving fast but it's not as seamless as home charging yet. Hybrids are simpler in this situation — you just fill up at any servo.
For long regional drives — think Brisbane to Sydney, Melbourne to Adelaide — the charging network has improved dramatically but still requires planning in a way that hybrids don't. If you do a lot of these drives regularly, a hybrid removes the friction.

The Incentives Gap
This is significant and often overlooked.
Full EVs qualify for:

FBT exemption on novated leases (saves $13k-$25k over 3 years)
State-based stamp duty exemptions in most states
State rebates where still available

Hybrids qualify for:

Nothing at the federal level
Limited state incentives — most have been wound back

PHEVs lost the FBT exemption for new leases from April 2025. So even plug-in hybrids no longer get the tax benefit that made them attractive on novated leases.

So Which One Should You Buy?
Go full EV if:

You have access to home charging
Your employer offers salary packaging — the novated lease maths are compelling
You do mostly city and suburban driving
You want the lowest possible running costs long term
You're in a state with stamp duty exemptions

Go hybrid if:

You live in an apartment without reliable charging access
You regularly drive long regional routes without good charging infrastructure
You want a simpler, more familiar ownership experience
The lower upfront cost matters more than long-term savings
You're not eligible for a novated lease

The honest truth: for most Australian employees in metro areas with home charging access, the full EV wins — especially on a novated lease. For everyone else, a hybrid is still a genuinely smart choice that saves money over a petrol car while removing the charging complexity.

The Bottom Line
The EV vs hybrid decision comes down to your charging situation, how you use the car, and whether you have access to a novated lease.
For most Australian employees in metro areas — the EV wins, and it's not particularly close once you factor in the novated lease tax benefit. For everyone else — a hybrid is still a genuinely smart upgrade over a petrol car.
