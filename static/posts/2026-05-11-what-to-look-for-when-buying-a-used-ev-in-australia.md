The used EV market in Australia is growing fast. Prices have dropped significantly from the early adoption era, which means you can now pick up a genuinely capable electric vehicle for well under $30,000 — if you know what you're looking at.

The problem is buying a used EV isn't the same as buying a used petrol car. There are a few specific things that can turn a bargain into a nightmare, and most buyers don't know to look for them. Here's what actually matters.

- - -

### The Battery — The Most Important Thing to Check

The battery is the heart of an EV and the one thing that's expensive to replace if something goes wrong. Here's what to check:

#### State of Health (SoH)

Most EVs display a battery health percentage somewhere in the settings or via an app. Anything above 85% is excellent for a car that's a few years old. Between 75-85% is still usable but you'll notice reduced range. Below 75% is worth negotiating hard on or walking away from.

For reference — real world data shows most EVs retain around 88-90% capacity after 5 years with normal use. If a car is showing significantly less than that, something has accelerated the degradation.

#### How to check it

* **Tesla** — the battery percentage shows in the app under vehicle health
* **BYD, Hyundai, Kia** — ask the dealer or previous owner for a health report, or take it to a dealer for a diagnostic check
* **Third party** — services like EV Battery Check Australia can do an independent battery health assessment for around $150-200. Worth it for any private sale over $20,000.

#### What degrades a battery faster

* Regular DC fast charging as the primary charging method
* Consistently charging to 100% and running to 0%
* Extended periods in extreme heat at high state of charge
* High odometer with no records of charging habits

### Check the Warranty — What's Left

Battery warranties in Australia typically run 8 years or 160,000km with a 70% capacity guarantee. For a used EV, check exactly how much warranty is left and whether it transfers to a new owner — most manufacturer warranties do transfer.

If a car is outside battery warranty and showing degradation below 80%, factor in potential replacement costs when negotiating the price.

- - -

### The FBT Exemption Eligibility Check

This is one most buyers miss entirely. If you're planning to use a novated lease on a used EV, the car must have been **first held and used after 1 July 2022** to qualify for the FBT exemption.

EVs registered before July 2022 are permanently ineligible — no matter how low the price is. Always check the first registration date before assuming a used EV qualifies for novated lease benefits.

Also check the original purchase price. If the car's first retail sale price exceeded the LCT threshold ($91,387 for fuel-efficient vehicles), it's permanently ineligible for the FBT exemption even if the current asking price is well below that.

### Charging History and Habits

Ask the seller how they charged the car. Ideally you want someone who:

* Charged primarily at home on AC overnight
* Kept daily charge between 20-80%
* Used DC fast chargers occasionally rather than regularly

There's no way to verify this completely but the battery health data will tell a story. A car with 60,000km and 82% battery health charged mostly at home. A car with 40,000km and 78% battery health was probably fast charged heavily.

- - -

### Software and Updates

EVs receive over-the-air software updates that improve performance, efficiency, and features. Check that the car is running the latest software version and that it's eligible for continued updates from the manufacturer.

Some older EVs from brands no longer operating in Australia (certain early Chinese imports) may have limited or no ongoing software support. Stick to established brands with a strong Australian presence.

- - -

### Check the Charging Equipment

Make sure the car comes with:

* The original AC charging cable (Mode 2 cable for standard power point)
* Any brand-specific adapters
* Confirmation the charge port is working correctly on both AC and DC

A missing or damaged charging cable can cost $300-600 to replace. Factor it into negotiations if it's not included.

- - -

### Service History

EVs need significantly less servicing than petrol cars — no oil changes, fewer brake services due to regenerative braking, no exhaust system. But they still need:

* Tyre rotations
* Brake fluid changes (typically every 2 years)
* Cabin air filter replacements
* Annual safety checks

Ask for service records. A car with no service history isn't automatically a problem given how little EVs need, but records give you peace of mind.

- - -

### Test Drive Checklist

On the test drive, look for:

* **Regenerative braking** — should feel smooth and consistent, not jerky
* **Acceleration** — should be immediate and linear with no hesitation
* **Charging port** — plug in and confirm it starts charging correctly
* **Climate control** — air con and heating working properly (these draw significant power)
* **Range display** — does the estimated range match what you'd expect based on the battery health?
* **Any warning lights** — any battery or drivetrain warnings are a red flag

### The Best Used EVs to Look for in Australia

**Tesla Model 3 (2019-2022)** — excellent reliability record, strong software support, good battery health data available. The most common used EV in Australia and well understood by mechanics.

**Nissan Leaf (2019-2022)** — affordable entry point but check battery health carefully. Earlier Leafs used passive air cooling which degrades faster in Australian heat. Later models improved but still worth checking.

**Hyundai Ioniq Electric (2019-2022)** — excellent real-world reliability, good battery retention, strong warranty support.

**Tesla Model Y (2022+)** — strong battery health, excellent software, great value as a used car given how quickly new ones depreciated.

**BYD Atto 3 (2022+)** — relatively new to the used market but strong early reliability data.

- - -

### Used EVs to Be Careful With

**Early Nissan Leaf (pre-2019)** — passive air cooling means higher degradation in Australian heat. Always get a battery health check.

**Early BMW i3** — quirky car with a small battery and limited range. Parts and service can be expensive.

**Unknown Chinese brands** — some early Chinese EV imports had limited dealer networks and no ongoing software support. Research the brand's current Australian presence before buying.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it worth buying a used EV in Australia?**

Yes — if you do the battery health check and confirm warranty status. The savings compared to new can be significant and EVs have far lower running costs than equivalent petrol cars.

**What's a good battery health percentage for a used EV?**

Above 85% is excellent. 75-85% is acceptable depending on age and price. Below 75% warrants either a significant price reduction or walking away.

**Can I get a used EV on a novated lease?**

Yes — but the car must have been first held and used after 1 July 2022 to qualify for the FBT exemption. Check the first registration date before proceeding.

**How do I check EV battery health in Australia?**

Most EVs display it in the settings or app. Third party services like EV Battery Check Australia offer independent assessments for around $150-200.

**Do used EVs come with warranty?**

Battery warranties typically transfer to new owners — check the exact terms for your brand. Most major manufacturers offer 8 year battery warranties that are transferable.

### The Bottom Line

Buying a used EV in Australia is a great way to get into electric driving at a lower cost — but the battery health check is non-negotiable. Get the state of health data, check warranty remaining, confirm FBT eligibility if you're planning a novated lease, and test drive properly.

Done right, a used EV is one of the best value purchases in the Australian car market right now.
